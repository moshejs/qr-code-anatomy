Every QR code you've ever scanned is quietly heroic. You point your phone at one that's been folded, printed on a crumpled receipt, half-covered by a coffee ring, or slapped with a sticker — and it just works. That resilience isn't luck. It's Reed–Solomon error correction, the same math that keeps CDs playing through scratches and spacecraft talking across the solar system, baked into a little black-and-white square.

What are all those cells doing?

Flip to Colour by role above and hover around. A QR code isn't a bitmap of your data — most of it is scaffolding the scanner needs to even find and orient the code:

The three big ■ finder squares in the corners let a camera lock on from any angle. The ■ timing lines running between them are a ruler — a strict alternating pattern that tells the scanner how big each cell is. Larger codes sprinkle in ■ alignment markers to fight lens distortion, and a ring of ■ format cells around the finders spells out which error-correction level and mask were used.

Only the ■ data cells carry your actual message. And right alongside them sits the hero of this whole story: the ■ parity cells.

The mask: why it looks like noise

A good QR code should be a roughly 50/50 salt-and-pepper mess. Big blank runs or accidental finder-lookalikes confuse scanners. So after laying down the data, the encoder XORs the whole thing with one of 8 checkerboard patterns — the mask — and keeps whichever result scores best against a set of penalty rules. That's why two nearly identical strings can produce wildly different-looking codes.

Reed–Solomon: the part that feels like magic

Here's the trick. Your message is chopped into 8-bit codewords — think of them as numbers from 0 to 255. Reed–Solomon treats those numbers as points and fits a polynomial through them, then writes down extra points from the same polynomial. Those extra points are the parity codewords.

Now damage the code. Some points get corrupted. But a polynomial of degree k is fully pinned down by any k good points — so as long as enough survive, the decoder can reconstruct the exact original curve and read off the true values. Even better, it doesn't need to be told which cells are wrong; the algebra locates the errors and fixes them.

Each level buys a different safety margin: L shrugs off ~7% damage, M ~15%, Q ~25%, and H survives roughly 30% of the code being obliterated. That's why the logo in the middle of a fancy QR code works — the designer just cranked the error correction up and let it eat the hole.

The decoder above is the genuine article: syndromes → Berlekamp–Massey → Chien search → Forney. When you knock out cells, it really is solving for the errors and telling you, live, whether your vandalism stayed inside the safety margin. Go find its breaking point.
